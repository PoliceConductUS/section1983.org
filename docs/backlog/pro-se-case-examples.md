# Pro Se Case Example Backlog

This backlog tracks real pro se examples that may become future teaching material.

The goal is to show useful patterns across the full range: strong pro se work, mixed filings, weak filings, and filings that teach a cautionary lesson. Turn an example into public-facing content only after the docket and available documents have been reviewed closely enough to keep the lesson accurate.

## Review Scale

- **Excellent**: a pro se filing or strategy worth modeling.
- **Solid**: mostly disciplined work with teachable gaps.
- **Mixed**: some usable choices and some choices that create avoidable risk.
- **Weak**: a filing that likely hides any viable issue behind pleading, service, proof, or tone problems.
- **Cautionary**: an extreme example that teaches what a court, clerk, defense lawyer, or judge sees first.

## Public-Use Rules

- Lead with the practical lesson and keep the tone disciplined.
- Treat a disorganized filing as a litigation-choice problem. Keep open the possibility that a valid claim is buried inside it.
- Separate the possible underlying grievance from the litigation choices shown in the docket.
- Identify the burden the filing creates for the plaintiff: proof, service, deadlines, credibility, and claim-by-claim structure.
- Use public docket facts and filed documents. If a document is unavailable, say so in the research notes.
- Before publishing any case study, check whether the case should also be added to the case library or whether the article should link only to the external docket.

## Candidate Examples

### James-Irvin v. Buffalo Police Department

- **Source**: <https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/68132435/james-irvin-v-buffalo-police-department/>
- **Court**: U.S. District Court for the Middle District of Florida
- **Docket number**: 2:23-cv-01237
- **Filed**: 2023-12-29
- **Terminated**: 2024-08-16
- **Nature shown by CourtListener**: 42:1983 Civil Rights Act; 440 Civil Rights: Other
- **Current teaching category**: Cautionary
- **Potential public angle**: "Wide feels powerful, but every defendant creates a burden."

#### Why This Belongs in the Backlog

The CourtListener metadata shows a pro se civil rights case with dozens of listed defendants, including police departments, government officials, media entities, entertainment figures, political figures, companies, and other unrelated parties.

The teaching point is that a very broad defendant list creates immediate burdens the plaintiff must carry. The plaintiffs may still have had a valid grievance inside a badly framed case.

For each defendant, the plaintiff must be able to answer:

1. What did this defendant personally do?
2. What constitutional right did that conduct violate?
3. How did that defendant's conduct cause the injury?
4. Can this defendant be sued under Section 1983 at all?
5. Can this defendant be served correctly and on time?

Even before the merits, service alone can become a major project. A complaint with dozens of defendants requires tracking summonses, waiver requests, addresses, proof of service, response deadlines, and any failed service attempts. For a pro se plaintiff, that workload can consume the case before the real claim is tested.

#### Possible Lesson for Future Article

A future article should teach this distinction:

- Filing against the "big and wide" set of people may feel like it captures the whole harm.
- The judge will still test each defendant one by one.
- The defense will use broad, unfocused pleading to argue that the plaintiff failed to connect facts to claims.
- The court may see the plaintiff as difficult to manage before it ever reaches any valid core claim.

Better public framing:

> Your complaint gets stronger when each defendant has a clear role, a clear act, a clear legal theory, and a clear path to service.

#### Research Notes Needed Before Publication

- Review the complaint and any available order terminating or dismissing the case.
- Confirm whether dismissal was based on screening, service, pleading defects, jurisdiction, failure to prosecute, or another ground.
- Check whether any claims were explained clearly enough to identify a potentially valid core issue.
- Avoid using unavailable attachments as support unless the document has been reviewed.
- Decide whether this should become a standalone cautionary article, a short example in an article on naming defendants, or a section in a broader "why pro se cases fail" article.
