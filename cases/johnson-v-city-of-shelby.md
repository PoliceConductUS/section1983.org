## What Happened

Two former City of Shelby police officers alleged that the city fired them because they exposed criminal activity by an alderman. They sought damages from the city for alleged Fourteenth Amendment due-process violations.

The lower courts entered judgment against them because their complaint omitted an express reference to § 1983. The Supreme Court reversed. [Read the opinion, 574 U.S. 10–12](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-574/pdf/USREPORTS-574-10.pdf).

## What the Court Decided

The complaint's factual account gave the city fair notice of the claim. The omission of the statutory citation was an insufficient reason to reject it. The Court also explained that qualified immunity was outside this city-only claim.

[Twombly](/cases/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly) and [Iqbal](/cases/ashcroft-v-iqbal) concern the factual allegations needed to state a plausible claim. The Court distinguished that requirement from the defect the lower courts identified here: an imperfect statement of the legal theory. It directed that the officers receive an opportunity to add the statutory reference.

## How You Can Use It

If the defense attacks your complaint because it omits the label “§ 1983,” point to the facts explaining the defendant's conduct, the right at issue, and the injury. [Johnson](/cases/johnson-v-city-of-shelby) supports judging that factual account under the ordinary pleading rules.

For your own draft, state the legal basis clearly and describe each defendant's responsibility. The decision helps with an omitted label; the complaint must still contain facts supporting the claim's requirements.

## What the Defense Will Say

The defense can accept that the statutory label is unnecessary and still argue that your facts fail to support liability. For a city claim, that includes the required connection to a policy or custom under [Monell](/cases/monell-v-dep-t-of-soc-servs).

Compare the defense's objection with the actual problem. A missing citation and a missing factual basis require different responses. The judge can reject a factually deficient complaint even though the statute is correctly named.
