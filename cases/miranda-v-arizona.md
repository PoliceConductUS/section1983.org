## What Happened

Ernesto Miranda was questioned in custody and signed a confession. The questioning proceeded without advice about his right to have a lawyer present. The confession was admitted at his trial, and he was convicted. The Supreme Court considered his case together with three other cases involving custodial questioning.

## What the Court Decided

The Court reversed Miranda's conviction. It required safeguards before custodial interrogation, including warnings about silence, the use of statements, and access to counsel. The prosecution bears the burden of showing a knowing, intelligent, and voluntary waiver when relying on one. [Read the opinion, 384 U.S. 444–445, 478–479, and 491–492](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep384/usrep384436/usrep384436.pdf).

## How to Use the Decision

Use [Miranda](/cases/miranda-v-arizona) to organize the sequence of custody, questioning, warnings, and any waiver. Preserve the recording and the exact words used. The judge will examine when the safeguards applied; the opposing side may argue that the statement was volunteered or that a valid waiver occurred.

For civil damages, apply [Vega v. Tekoh](/cases/vega-v-tekoh) separately. A violation of Miranda's warning rules alone supplies no § 1983 claim. Read the [Miranda concept guide](/terms-and-concepts/miranda) for that distinction and the recognized exceptions.
