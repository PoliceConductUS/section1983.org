## What This Case Is About

Daphne Hicks sued the City of Irving and seven police officers after two entries into her apartments. Judge Jorge A. Solis dismissed her claims in a November 13, 2012 order. The city claims lacked specific facts connecting a city policy to her injuries, and the court treated her challenges to the officers' searches and warrants as an attack on her marijuana conviction. (Order at 9–12.)

For your own case, the useful distinction is between the reason each claim failed and the facts Hicks alleged. The court decided [motions to dismiss](/terms-and-concepts/motion-to-dismiss), applying rules about the complaint and legal obstacles to relief. Its background section recounts allegations from Hicks' filings. (Order at 1–6.)

## The Facts

### The May 5, 2011 entry

According to the allegations summarized in the order, Officers Jonathan Rowan and Robert Turner responded to an anonymous disturbance call at Hicks' apartment. She alleged that they knocked out her door's peephole, threatened to break down the door, and forced their way inside when it opened slightly. The order identifies a dispute about whether the officers smelled marijuana before or after the door opened. (Order at 2 & n.3.)

Hicks alleged that officers detained her and a guest for four to five hours. During that time, she said, officers forced her to undress in their view, monitored her use of the restroom, and prevented her from speaking. She alleged that Sergeant Kirk LeCroy later arrived wearing fatigue pants, a T-shirt, and a ski mask and repeatedly touched his holstered pistol while questioning them. (Order at 2.)

The order recounts the issuance of a drug-trafficking search warrant and the seizure of money, electronics, and other property. It also notes the later release of most of the property, followed by the release of her graphic-design workstation. (Order at 2–3 & n.5.)

### The June 23, 2011 entry and the conviction

Hicks alleged that Officers Jonathan Long, Douglas Holbert, and Jay Montanez entered her new apartment with guns drawn to serve arrest and search warrants. She said they entered her bedroom while she was sleeping nude and grabbed and handcuffed her. She also challenged police reports and warrant documents as fabricated. (Order at 3, 11–12 & n.10.)

The court relied on a criminal judgment showing that a jury convicted Hicks of possessing more than four ounces of marijuana on June 21, 2012. The order describes a two-year state-jail sentence, suspended for five years of probation. (Order at 12.)

## What the Court Decided

### The court limited which pleadings it would consider

The court considered Federal Petitions I and II because the defendants had addressed them in their dismissal motions. It rejected further proposed pleadings and additional defendants, finding that the changes would still fail to state a claim and would unfairly burden the defense. It then assessed the claims under Rule 12(b)(6), which asks whether the complaint contains facts supporting a claim for relief. (Order at 3–6 & n.6.)

### The city claims needed facts connecting a city policy to the injury

Under [_Monell v. Department of Social Services_](/cases/monell-v-dep-t-of-soc-servs), a city's own policy or custom must cause the constitutional injury. The court applied [_Piotrowski v. City of Houston_](/cases/piotrowski-v-city-of-houston), which requires a policymaker, a policy or custom, and a direct causal connection to the violation. (Order at 9–10.)

Hicks alleged that Irving permitted civil-rights violations and covered up relevant documents. The court found that these assertions lacked facts identifying the policymaker, the policy or custom, and how it caused her injury. It dismissed the federal claims against Irving with prejudice, meaning Hicks could not simply refile those claims. (Order at 10–11.)

### The officers' federal claims were dismissed under the [_Heck_](/cases/heck-v-humphrey) rule

The officers argued for [qualified immunity](/terms-and-concepts/qualified-immunity). The court resolved the federal claims against them under [_Heck v. Humphrey_](/cases/heck-v-humphrey), which addresses damages claims that would invalidate an existing conviction or sentence. (Order at 11–12.)

The court treated Hicks' challenges to probable cause and allegedly false warrants as an attack on her marijuana conviction. Her conviction remained in place, and she had supplied no evidence of a reversal or other qualifying invalidation. The order dismissed these claims “with prejudice until such time as the requirements of Heck are satisfied.” That condition matters: the order expressly tied this dismissal to the status of the conviction. (Order at 12.)

### Separate rules defeated the remaining claims

- **Claims based directly on criminal statutes:** The court found that the criminal provisions Hicks invoked gave her no private civil claim. It dismissed those claims with prejudice. (Order at 6.)
- **State-law claims against Irving:** The court applied governmental immunity, which protects a government from suit unless the law permits the claim. It found that the Texas Tort Claims Act's exceptions for police protection and intentional torts left Irving immune from these claims, including negligence allegations arising from the same conduct. (Order at 6–8.)
- **State-law claims against the officers:** Hicks sued both Irving and its employees over the same events. Irving moved to dismiss the employees under Texas Civil Practice and Remedies Code § 101.106(e), and the court granted that motion. This ruling concerned the state-law claims; the court addressed the federal claims separately. (Order at 8.)
- **Eighth Amendment claims:** The court found that Hicks' allegations about the two incidents supplied no factual basis for an Eighth Amendment claim concerning incarceration. It dismissed those claims with prejudice. (Order at 8–9.)

The conclusion dismissed all claims against all defendants with prejudice. Read that conclusion together with the specific condition attached to the officers' federal claims on page 12. (Order at 12–13.)

## Why This Case Matters for Your § 1983 Case

**Match each defense to a particular claim.** For a claim against a city, the defense will ask which policy caused your injury and who was responsible for it. Identify those facts in your complaint and explain the causal connection. Read the [municipal-liability guide](/terms-and-concepts/monell-liability) for that separate task.

**Compare your civil claims with the criminal judgment.** The judge will ask whether winning a particular damages claim would necessarily imply that the conviction is invalid. Identify the conviction, the conduct each claim challenges, and any order changing the conviction's status. Use the [Heck doctrine guide](/terms-and-concepts/heck-doctrine) to examine that connection claim by claim.

**Separate state-law and federal claims.** In this order, the Texas election-of-remedies rule removed the officers from the state-law claims on Irving's motion. The federal claims received their own analysis. Keep those legal grounds distinct when reading a defense motion or drafting your response.

## Key Takeaway

The dismissal turned on several different grounds: insufficient facts about city responsibility, a conviction that remained in place, and separate limits on the remaining claims. Use the order to identify the defense to each of your claims, the facts needed to answer it, and the precise scope of any dismissal.
