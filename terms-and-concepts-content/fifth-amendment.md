## What It Protects

The Fifth Amendment protects you against being compelled to provide testimonial evidence that could incriminate you. **Testimonial** evidence communicates information, such as an answer about what happened or what you know. A physical characteristic, such as how your voice sounds, raises a different question from the information your words convey. [Pennsylvania v. Muniz](/cases/pennsylvania-v-muniz) illustrates that distinction.

You can assert the privilege during official questioning when an answer could incriminate you in a future criminal proceeding. The protection can matter before charges exist and outside police custody. [Vega v. Tekoh](/cases/vega-v-tekoh) explains that scope before distinguishing the privilege from Miranda's warning rules.

This page focuses on self-incrimination during police or jail questioning. The Fifth Amendment also contains grand-jury, double-jeopardy, due-process, and takings provisions. Read the [amendment's text](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/bill-of-rights-transcript#toc-amendment-v) for those separate protections.

## What You Should Be Able to Identify

For your own record, separate three questions:

- What information did the official seek, and how could it incriminate you?
- What pressure or penalty was used to obtain it?
- How was the answer used, and what remedy are you seeking?

The judge needs the particular question, the setting, and the claimed harm. A general statement that officers violated your right to silence leaves those questions unanswered.

## How This Differs from Miranda

[Miranda](/terms-and-concepts/miranda) generally requires warnings before **custodial interrogation**: questioning in circumstances involving arrest or comparable restraint. Those safeguards protect the privilege against self-incrimination.

For a § 1983 damages claim, identify the constitutional violation separately. Under [Vega](/cases/vega-v-tekoh), a violation of Miranda's warning rules alone supplies no § 1983 claim, including when the unwarned statement was admitted at trial. A claim involving actual compulsion requires analysis of the pressure, the testimonial information, its criminal use, and the applicable law. [Read the opinion, pages 4 and 13–16](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/21pdf/21-499_gfbh.pdf).

## What the Defense Will Say

The defense may characterize the answer as voluntary, the requested evidence as physical rather than testimonial, or your complaint as a warning-only claim. For booking questions, it may describe the exchange as routine collection of identifying information.

Compare that description with the recording. Write down the exact question, why the answer could incriminate you, what happened after you declined to answer, and where the answer later appeared. A booking label calls for examining the question's function and context. [Muniz](/cases/pennsylvania-v-muniz) discusses both administrative questions and a question that called for an incriminating testimonial response.
