## What You Are Looking For

The [Fourth Amendment](/terms-and-concepts/fourth-amendment) governs government searches and seizures. Searching your home or pockets, stopping your car, arresting you, and taking your belongings can involve different intrusions. Identify each one before deciding what justification the officer needed.

A search can involve an intrusion into protected privacy or a physical intrusion into a constitutionally protected place or thing to obtain information. A seizure of a person involves restraint; a seizure of property involves meaningful interference with possession. The amendment requires reasonableness, with the governing rule depending on the particular intrusion. The [U.S. Courts' overview](https://www.uscourts.gov/about-federal-courts/educational-resources/about-educational-outreach/activity-resources/what-does-fourth-amendment-mean) explains common settings.

## What You Should Be Able to Separate

List what happened in sequence. For each action, identify its time, place, scope, and the justification offered.

| Action                                       | Question to examine                                                                       |
| -------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Brief investigative stop                     | What facts supported reasonable suspicion, and did the stop stay within its proper scope? |
| Pat-down for weapons                         | What supported a reasonable belief that the person was armed and dangerous?               |
| Arrest                                       | What facts known at that time supported probable cause?                                   |
| Search of a home, belongings, or information | What warrant or recognized exception authorized this search, and what were its limits?    |
| Taking or retaining property                 | What justified interfering with possession, and for how long?                             |

## A Stop and a Frisk Need Separate Justifications

[Terry v. Ohio](/cases/terry-v-ohio) addressed both a brief stop and a limited weapons frisk. Facts supporting investigation of possible crime and facts supporting a belief that someone is armed and dangerous answer different questions. A lawful stop alone leaves the justification for a frisk to be examined. [Read Terry, pages 20–22 and 27–30](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep392/usrep392001/usrep392001.pdf).

The same method helps with a longer encounter. An arrest, a vehicle search, and a later home entry should each have their own place in your timeline.

## What the Defense Will Say

The defense may rely on a warrant, consent, an emergency, or another exception suited to the search. Ask which rule it invokes and what facts bring this particular action within that rule. For consent, preserve the exact request, answer, and scope of the permission claimed.

Collect the warrant and supporting affidavit if available, reports, dispatch logs, video, and property records. An **affidavit** is a sworn factual statement; for a warrant, it helps show what information the issuing judge received.

If you seek damages, the defense may also assert [qualified immunity](/terms-and-concepts/qualified-immunity). If you have an existing conviction, separately examine whether success on your particular civil claim would necessarily show that it is invalid under [Heck v. Humphrey](/cases/heck-v-humphrey). Each question can affect whether the claim proceeds.
